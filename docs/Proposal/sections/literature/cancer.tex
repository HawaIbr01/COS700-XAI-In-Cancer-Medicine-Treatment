\label{ssub:cancer}

Cancer is a mixed group of diseases that can be haematologically (blood) related or tumour related
\cite{NCI}. They are all characterised by uncontrolled cellular growth triggered by genetic
mutations and epigenetic alterations driven by environmental factors \cite{NCI}. Traditionally,
treatment of cancer relied on generalised treatment protocols that were based on the anatomical site
of the cancer. Modern protocols have shifted to what can be termed precision medicine, which is aimed
at providing customised individual patient care. Precision pathology in particular aims to tailor
treatment to patients based on the individual molecular profile of the patient's cancer
\cite{Klauschen2024}. Achieving this requires the integration of high-dimensional genomic data, such
as gene expression profiles (transcriptomics), to predict how a specific cancer will respond to
targeted therapies \cite{Klauschen2024}.
\\
Precision pathology has evolved from manual microscopic inspection methods to AI-driven diagnostics.
Machine learning models are now capable of analysing complex biological datasets to predict patient
survival outcomes and drug sensitivities \cite{Kovalev2021}. A particular case is that of machine
learning survival models. Machine learning survival models are predictive models designed to estimate
time-to-event outcomes, for example how long a patient is likely to live after diagnosis and/or
treatment \cite{Kovalev2021}. This is useful in determining the potency of a treatment plan based on
the patient's characteristics \cite{Kovalev2021}.

\subsubsection{Genomics and the Interpretability Gap}
\label{ssub:genomics-interpretability}
The precision of machine learning models in precision medicine is driven by massive datasets such as
the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC). In genomic-based cancer research, datasets like
GDSC provide thousands of gene expression variables \cite{Baptista2021,Yang2013}. The GDSC dataset is
a large publicly available database that is designed to help researchers discover therapeutic biomarkers (e.g. the expression level of the \textit{EGFR} gene, a receptor gene
frequently overexpressed or mutated in tumours and a common therapeutic target) and predict how
specific tumours will respond
to various cancer treatments \cite{Yang2013}. It is practically, as well as financially, infeasible to
test every candidate drug directly on human patients. Therefore, cancer cell lines are used as a proxy
for real tumours \cite{Yang2013}. Cancer cell lines are cells that have been extracted and grown in a
laboratory and still retain the genetic and molecular characteristics of the cancers they originate
from \cite{Yang2013}. Drug response is quantified using the half-maximal inhibitory concentration
(IC$_{50}$), the drug concentration required to reduce the viability of a cell population by 50\%, so
that a lower IC$_{50}$ indicates a more sensitive (more easily killed) cell line \cite{Yang2013}.
Because raw IC$_{50}$ values span several orders of magnitude, their natural logarithm (LN IC$_{50}$)
is commonly used as the continuous regression target, and it is the response metric adopted throughout
this study.
\\
While DNNs excel at finding patterns in high-dimensional datasets such as GDSC, they largely operate
as black boxes \cite{Partin2023,Klauschen2024}. To elaborate, current research has successfully used
AI to identify biomarkers that correlate with treatment resistance or treatment efficacy
\cite{Partin2023,Klauschen2024,Baptista2021}. Despite this success in predictive accuracy, a
significant trust gap exists, which is that clinicians are not able to easily verify whether a model's
prediction is based on genuine oncogenic pathways or statistical noise \cite{Klauschen2024}. Klauschen
et al.~\cite{Klauschen2024} argue that for AI to be integrated into clinical workflows, it must
provide ``explainable'' outputs that align with established medical knowledge.
\\
In XAI, unreliability of explanations is related to data corruption or noise \cite{Klauschen2024}. In
cancer genomics, small variations in the sample preparation can lead to batch effects which AI models
learn as shortcuts to high accuracy, or Clever Hans \cite{Partin2023,Klauschen2024}. Batch effects are
non-biological, technical variations that are introduced into the data \cite{Yu2024,Goh2022}. Batch
effects are not related to the variables of interest in the study and come about due to data being
collected from different labs or machines, different assessment pipelines, or differing experimental
conditions over time \cite{Yu2024}.
\\
Research on machine learning survival models shows that black-box models can produce highly accurate
prediction scores while hiding the underlying counterfactuals: specific changes in a patient's genetic
expression that can alter the clinical outcome or decision \cite{Kovalev2021}. Furthermore, if an XAI
method identifies a ``rule'' for a cancer diagnosis that is mathematically sound but biologically
incoherent (e.g. incorrectly linking a housekeeping gene to tumour suppression), it can lead to
dangerous medical misinterpretations \cite{Partin2023,Klauschen2024}. This underscores the need for
the rigorous ``Audit and Verify'' approach proposed in this research project to ensure that
interpretability serves as a safeguard for clinical truth rather than just a justification for model
output.
